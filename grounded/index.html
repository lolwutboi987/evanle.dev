Beyond the story

Field notes.

A closer look at the beliefs, rituals, and contradictions of the Grounded Movement. Read the full papers and community code, including the social justice analysis and artist’s statement.

The rule of the tool

Human authorship is sacred; there is no creator god. Grounded permits electricity, ordinary computers, tractors, refrigeration, recorded music, and medicine administered by human practitioners.

A word processor may record a sentence; it may not compose one. A music program may record a performance; it may not generate the melody. Tools may perform specified operations, but may not infer desires, select life goals, or regulate emotions on someone’s behalf.

“Deterministic tools” is doctrinal shorthand, not a precise technical definition. Medical AI, adaptive prosthetics, and reproductive technology expose the cost of an absolute ban. Maya supports exceptions when a tool makes participation possible. Purists fear every exception leads back to dependence.

A life built together

The First Making. Newcomers present a work alongside its drafts: a joke, a melody, a drawing, or a useful object. Over time, elders demand evidence of prolonged effort.

The Witness. Weekly gatherings encounter works as they were made. Laughter, disagreement, surprise, and grief return. Some Circles eventually exclude works that question doctrine.

The Account. Members disclose how their work was produced. Skill-sharing hardens into public confession. Hidden AI use can cost teaching privileges or membership.

Common Work. Farming, maintenance, childcare, and teaching are shared according to ability. Local councils called Circles govern, assisted by Keepers of Tools, Memory, and Witness. Positions rotate in principle; Maya’s writings become a source of authority for elders.

The cost of belonging

Caretaker’s material achievements are real. Grounded reveals no secret famine or exploited underclass. Its objection concerns control over meaning. But its own elders begin deciding which expression others may encounter.

Restrictions on adaptive technology exclude some bodies. Removing automation can place unequal care burdens on women. Communal land and workshops depend on good standing. Members may leave, but departure can mean losing daily contact with family and community.

Grounded welcomes every race, gender, and sexuality. Yet elders can privilege some cultural traditions as more “authentic” than others. A movement that promises freedom must confront whether that freedom includes people who reject its doctrine.

The Grounded writings

II. The Unfinished Work

Leave room for the hand to hesitate.
Leave room for the thought to change.

III. The Warning

Do not ask the machine what you mean.
Make something.
Let another person answer.

IV. The Disputed Passage

No one owes us their suffering.
We owe one another room to grow.

The artist’s statement

Read the full artist’s statement and sources for the connection to Parable of the Talents, Afrofuturism, Latinx Futurism, and Indigenous Futurisms.

This story explores what happens when a society eliminates suffering so successfully that people gradually surrender the ability to shape their lives. Caretaker’s citizens are happy. Its achievements deserve to survive.

Maya does not discover that machines produce ugly work. She discovers that perfect satisfaction has displaced the uncertain exchange between a maker and an audience. Art can reveal a desire before its maker fully understands it.

The connection to Octavia Butler’s Parable novels is the tension between belief, community, and power: a founder’s philosophy can help people build a shared life while her authority threatens their freedom.

The story does not argue that pain is necessary for good art, or that people should lose medicine, peace, or reproductive freedom. Its final test is whether a movement can defend an artist whose work makes its own followers uncomfortable.
