The Crisis of U.S. Energy Consumption

The core mission of this project is to bridge the gap between complex environmental data and everyday human behavior. In the United States, energy consumption represents a monumental environmental hurdle: despite making up less than 5% of the global population, the U.S. consumes roughly 16% of the world's energy. The vast majority of this electricity is still generated by burning fossil fuels like natural gas and coal, making the power sector one of the nation's primary drivers of greenhouse gas emissions and climate change.

The problem is further compounded by two invisible factors: grid inefficiency and peak demand. Due to thermodynamic limits and aging infrastructure, nearly two-thirds of the energy created at power plants is completely lost as waste heat before it ever reaches a household light switch. Furthermore, during periods of peak demand—such as hot summer afternoons when millions of air conditioners turn on simultaneously—utilities are forced to fire up backup "peaker plants." These auxiliary plants are usually the oldest, least efficient, and most polluting fossil-fuel facilities on the grid.

The Structural Challenges of the American Grid

The United States grid is not a single cohesive network, but rather a fragmented patchwork divided into three major independent interconnections: the Eastern Interconnection, the Western Interconnection, and Texas (ERCOT). This fragmented design severely limits the country's ability to transmit clean energy from regions where it is abundantly generated to urban centers where demand is highest. For example, wind energy generated during overnight storms in the Great Plains often cannot reach coastal cities due to bottlenecks in long-distance, high-voltage transmission lines.

Furthermore, our heavy reliance on fossil fuels introduces severe environmental externalities beyond global warming. The extraction of coal via mountaintop removal destroys localized carbon sinks and causes severe acid mine drainage, polluting nearby watersheds with heavy metals. Burning these fossil fuels releases criteria air pollutants under the Clean Air Act—including sulfur dioxide ($SO_2$), nitrogen oxides ($NO_x$), and particulate matter ($PM_{2.5}$)—which directly leads to photochemical smog, industrial acid deposition, and chronic respiratory diseases in surrounding human populations.

Because these processes happen entirely behind the scenes, greenhouse gases remain abstract to the average consumer. This project tackles this ongoing interaction by targeting a major carbon source we interact with every single second of our lives: household and regional electricity consumption.

To make this urgent issue highly accessible, the project takes the form of an interactive digital map hosted at evanle.dev. Instead of relying on dense academic text or confusing utility bills, the website uses intuitive, visual mapping to allow everyday users to see exactly where their energy comes from, how clean or dirty their local grid is at any given hour, and the immediate environmental toll of their daily routines. By keeping the language simple, jargon-free, and visually driven, the platform transforms intimidating climate data into an engaging learning experience for the general public.

Research and Data Foundation

To ensure the project is scientifically sound and accurate, the map integrates real-time, verified data directly from three major authoritative institutions via public APIs:

  • The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA): Provides live data on grid composition, showing the breakdown of energy sources (fossil fuels vs. renewables) powering different regions. It tracks the real-time consumption of gigawatt-hours ($GWh$) across balancing authorities.
  • The Environmental Protection Agency (EPA): Supplies localized emissions data to calculate the exact carbon intensity associated with that regional energy use, referencing eGRID subregion emission factors to convert fuel use into pounds of $CO_2$ equivalent per megawatt-hour.
  • The U.S. Census Bureau: Offers demographic and geographic data to contextualize energy consumption patterns across different populations, revealing how residential density impacts localized structural demand.

By combining these three distinct sources, the map provides users with a comprehensive, localized look at the invisible infrastructure behind their light switches.

Solutions and a Message of Hope

Rather than leaving users discouraged by the scale of energy-related emissions, the platform is designed to inspire action through actionable solutions and systemic hope:

  • Individual Empowerment: The tool helps users identify peak high-emissions times in their specific region. This knowledge empowers them to shift high-energy tasks—like running dishwashers, doing laundry, or charging electric vehicles—to off-peak hours when the grid relies more heavily on clean, renewable energy. This practice, known as demand-side management, actively lessens the need for dirtier peaker plants.
  • Systemic Progress: By visualizing the data, the map highlights regions that have successfully integrated wind, solar, and hydro-power. This serves as a tangible proof-of-concept, showing users that a transition to a low-carbon grid is not a distant dream, but a reality already unfolding across the country through macro-level improvements like utility-scale battery storage installations and smart grid automation.

Ultimately, the evanle.dev energy map turns passive energy consumers into conscious, informed participants in the clean energy transition.
